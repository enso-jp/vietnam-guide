## Comprehensive list of local food to try

### Com tam
- Vietnamese name: Cơm tấm
- What it is: S8 says it is one of the few dishes that originated here, broken rice that farmers once kept because the grains were not sold, eaten with honey-glazed barbecued pork chops, a sunny-side-up egg, spring onions, and pickled papaya. S10 calls it the essential Saigon dish: marinated pork ribs grilled over low heat, on broken rice, with an omelet, egg rolls, and sausages, and onion fat or pork grease, plus fresh and pickled vegetables. The plates are not the same list. Keep both. Do not invent a third.
- How to eat it: S8 says pour a generous amount of sweet fish sauce over the plate. S10 says add the vegetables. No other method was printed.
- When to order it: S8 says it is the go-to breakfast and that people also eat it at any meal. S10 says the smell is on the street at all hours.
- Area, not a shop: All over the center (S10). S8 tells the reader to use the nearest room and names one shop only as a clue. See Notes.
- Meal: breakfast
- Neighbor dish to link: none on another city’s plate. The dish slug `com-tam` is already this city’s.
- Licensed photo: Yes. Commons `File:Cơm tấm SG (cơm sườn) ng27th9n2020 (1).jpg`, CC0, opened 22 Oct 2026. Rice, a chop, cucumber, scallions. No egg in the frame. Not in the repo. A Da Nang file from the same search was not used.
- Sources: S8, S10

### Banh mi
- Vietnamese name: Bánh mì
- What it is: Two compositions. S8: char siu, homemade pâté, soft butter, pickled papaya, and hot chili, and some cooks add a sauce. S10: pâté and chili sauce, then meats, fresh herbs, and pickled vegetables, usually carrot and daikon. S10 says the baguette is the center of the dish and that the sandwich is said to have originated in this city. S16 says pickled vegetables and pork. All three stay.
- How to eat it: S10 says it is eaten in the hand, at any hour, and that the sauce ties the salty, sweet, and spicy flavors. That taste line is S10’s, not a tasting note added here.
- When to order it: S10 says any time of day. S16 uses it as the start of a morning.
- Area, not a shop: S1 says the food stalls at the end of the Ben Thanh hall sell freshly assembled bánh mì. S11 names bánh mì on Bùi Viện. Shops in Notes are not the area.
- Meal: breakfast
- Neighbor dish to link: `banh-mi` is already shared. No second city was given a different recipe on these pages beyond “prepared very differently in different parts of Vietnam” (S10).
- Licensed photo: Yes. In the repo, Minh Nguyen, CC BY-SA 4.0, `File:Bánh mì thịt nguội.jpg`. A filled baguette. Opened 22 Oct 2026.
- Sources: S1, S8, S10, S11, S16

### Hu tieu
- Vietnamese name: Hủ tiếu
- What it is: S8 says few dishes represent the city as this one does, with Chinese, Cambodian, and Vietnamese origins. Hủ tiếu Nam Vang: thin rice noodles in a hot bone broth, shrimp, pork, quail eggs, fried garlic. Hủ tiếu Sa Đéc: from the former Sa Đéc province, with a sweet sauce S8 calls a Delta signature, a chewier noodle, and a plate of bean sprouts and lettuce. S10: broth simmered with pork bones, dried squid, shrimp, and root vegetables; “delicate, slightly sweet, clear”; toppings of boiled eggs, shrimp, and pork as ground meat, lean meat, liver, intestines, and tongue. Dry hủ tiếu comes with the broth on the side (S10). The two pages do not list the same toppings. Keep both.
- How to eat it: S10 says add lettuce, bean sprouts, and scallions. The dry bowl is eaten with the broth beside it.
- When to order it: S8 says the Nam Vang bowl is on carts all over the city. A clock was not printed. Check on arrival — UNSOURCED.
- Area, not a shop: Carts across the city (S8). S14’s night-market article was not opened, so a Bình Tây stall claim from a search snippet is not used.
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: `hu-tieu-my-tho` is a different dish page. Link it so copy does not treat that Mỹ Tho bowl as this one. `pho` is the comparison S10 makes, not the same soup.

### Oc
- Vietnamese name: Ốc
- What it is: S8 calls snails one of the city’s most famous specialties. Restaurants lay the day’s catch out in baskets, up to twenty kinds. S8 names sweet snails, cockles, and razor clams, cooked stir-fried with butter and garlic, grilled with sea salt and chili, or stewed in coconut milk. S10 says they are boiled, steamed, grilled, or sautéed and eaten from the shell with a cold beer. S16’s District 4 spread adds crab, scallops, molluscs, and freshwater snails in butter, garlic, lemongrass, and chili. The lists overlap and do not match. Keep all three.
- How to eat it: S10 says one by one from the shell. S8 says ask what the next table is eating if the baskets are too many.
- When to order it: S8 says Vĩnh Khánh comes to life just after dark, around 6 p.m., weekdays and weekends. S9 describes the same street as an evening party where people grill meat and seafood at the table.
- Area, not a shop: Vĩnh Khánh, District 4 (S8, S9). Also the Nhiêu Lộc–Thị Nghè bank (S8) and Nguyễn Thượng Hiền in District 3 for clams, sea snails, crab claws, and oysters (S9).
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: none
- Licensed photo: Unpublished: no licensed photo. A frame of the baskets was not opened.
- Sources: S8, S9, S10, S16
