## Comprehensive list of local food to try

### Cao lau
- Vietnamese name: Cao lầu
- What it is: Hoi An’s noodle, which the heritage centre’s 11 Aug 2025 article says people here call bánh, not mì. Thick rice noodles from local Xuyệt rice, soaked with wood-ash water from tràm on Cù Lao Chàm, milled with water from the Bá Lễ well, steamed, rolled, and cut. Served with a little pork broth, char siu, crispy ram, and Trà Quế herbs: húng lủi, young mustard, rau đắng, diếp cá (S28). It looks related to mì Quảng and is not that bowl (S28).
- How to eat it: Toss it while it is hot. The broth wets the noodles. It is not a soup you drink (S28).
- When to order it: Hot. The article does not lock it to one hour. Lunch is the natural bowl. A cold takeaway is the miss the same article warns against (S28).
- Area, not a shop: The old town. A 7 Jun 2023 note on the heritage site names the food hall inside Chợ Hội An and the night market on Nguyễn Hoàng, Công nữ Ngọc Hoa, Trần Quý Cáp, and Bạch Đằng (S31). Shop names and the prices on that note stay under Notes, not pages.
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: mì Quảng, the Da Nang and Quảng bowl, which this one is not (S28, S30)
- Sources: S28, S30, S31

### White rose dumpling
- Vietnamese name: Bánh bao bánh vạc / bánh bông hồng trắng
- What it is: A steamed dumpling the heritage centre traces to Hoa families in the port, from a Chinese há cảo, filled so the white bánh bao looks like a rose. Eaten with bánh vạc. The English name “white rose” is the story on that page: a foreign guest in the early 1990s could not remember the Vietnamese name and said white rose (S29). Vietnam Tourism says the wrapper is rice flour and the same well water as cao lầu, filled with minced pork, shrimp, and mushrooms, with fried scallion and shallot (S30). The heritage article describes a thin wrapper, shrimp-and-pork filling, and fried shallot, dipped in sweet-sour fish sauce, eaten warm (S29). Where the two pages disagree on the filling, keep both: S29 says tôm thịt; S30 adds mushrooms.
- How to eat it: One dumpling is one bite. Dip. Eat it warm. A cold plate misses the wrapper (S29).
- When to order it: A small plate, not the whole lunch. The heritage article calls it a refined dish, historically expensive, now on local-specialty menus (S29).
- Area, not a shop: The old streets where it was carried: Lê Lợi, Trần Phú, Nguyễn Thái Học, Bạch Đằng (S29). The family workshop named on that page is under Notes, not pages.
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: none
- Sources: S29, S30

### Com ga
- Vietnamese name: Cơm gà
- What it is: Chicken rice. Vietnam Tourism says the Hoi An plate is believed to come from a Hainanese dish brought by Chinese immigrants, and that here the chicken is shredded, the rice is yellow with chicken broth and turmeric, and the sides are herbs, a bowl of chicken soup, and a sour papaya salad (S30).
- How to eat it: Eat the chicken with the rice and the salad. The soup is beside the plate (S30).
- When to order it: A lunch when you do not want noodles (S30).
- Area, not a shop: Hoi An. The shops named on S30 are not published as picks. The heritage food note also names cơm gà as a local bowl without a street (S31).
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: the Da Nang com ga page already separates a Hong Kong-style plate from this Hoi An plate. Do not rewrite those Da Nang shops.
- Sources: S30, S31

### Banh dap
- Vietnamese name: Bánh đập
- What it is: A cracker you break by hand. - How to eat it: Press the middle so the crisp sheet shatters onto the soft sheet. Dip, or spoon the sauce over.
- When to order it: A snack, often with hến xào. Clock: Check on arrival — UNSOURCED.
- Area, not a shop: Cẩm Nam (S45, S48). Shop names stay under Notes. `do not publish as a pick`.

- Sources: S45, S47, S48, S51

### Hoanh thanh
- Vietnamese name: Hoành thánh
- What it is: A wonton the conservation centre’s 28 Jun 2011 article (S49, citing vntravellive.com) treats as a Chinese port dish with three forms: fried, soup, and with thin wheat noodles. Wrapper of wheat flour and egg. Filling on that article is pounded land shrimp. Soup broth is pork bone, kept clear, with straw mushrooms, cabbage, and pineapple; seven to ten dumplings in a bowl; satay and crushed fried pork crackling on the side. Fried ones are folded square, set on lettuce, tomato, and Trà Quế herbs, with a tomato-and-potato sauce. The article also prints a Qianlong naming story and says Quảng Nam says hoành thánh while some northern speakers say mằn thắn or vằn thắn. S46 says a golden soft wrapper, a shrimp-and-pork filling, pork liver, garlic chives, and a soft-yolk egg, or a fried plate with chili sauce. S44 only says to try the fried one as a variation. Keep the shrimp filling and the shrimp-and-pork filling.
- How to eat it: Soup with a lot of broth. Fried as a dry plate. Noodles hot.
- When to order it: A meal or a starter. Clock: Check on arrival — UNSOURCED.
- Area, not a shop: Old town. Shop names and the 20,000–50,000 VND line on S47 are single-source and stay under Notes. `do not publish as a pick`.
- Meal: main
- Neighbor dish to link: none
- Sources: S44, S46, S47, S49
